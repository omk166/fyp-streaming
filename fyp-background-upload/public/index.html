      The three modes

      • Off (raw) turns the browser's echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control off. Others hear your microphone as captured: the baseline for comparison. If someone uses speakers, you may hear yourself echo back from them.
      • Browser (built-in) uses the browser's own processing (in Chrome and Edge: the WebRTC echo canceller AEC3, WebRTC noise suppression and automatic gain control). The two switches turn its echo cancellation and noise suppression on or off; gain control stays on. This was the app's behaviour before this panel existed.
      • Ours turns all browser processing off, so audio is never processed twice, then runs our own pipeline on this device: microphone → DTLN-aec 128 (echo, if on) → DeepFilterNet3 (noise, if on) → the call. The echo canceller is given the exact audio this page plays: other participants, plus the sound of a tab you share.

      Why these models

      Echo: DTLN-aec 128 (Westhausen & Meyer, ICASSP 2021). Reported results against the WebRTC AEC3 baseline: ERLE 34.8 dB vs 24.2 dB, STOI 0.772 vs 0.628, with 24 ms output delay. We use the 128-unit model because the 512-unit one needs about 21× the compute. Noise: DeepFilterNet3 (Schröter et al., 2023). Reported PESQ 2.18 and STOI 0.888 on the DNS Challenge 2020 remix test set, at about 25× the cost of WebRTC noise suppression and 3× DTLN's. These are published figures, not measurements made by this project; the test page measures your machine.

      Trade-offs

      • Latency: DTLN-aec delays audio by 24 ms by design; DeepFilterNet3, resampling and buffering add more. The panel shows the measured total while Ours runs.
      • CPU: both models run on your device. The panel shows processing time per frame; if a slow machine cannot keep up, dropped audio is reported, not hidden.
      • Bandwidth: DTLN-aec only exists at 16 kHz, so with echo cancellation on your voice is limited to 8 kHz. Noise suppression alone keeps the full 48 kHz band.
      • Speech recognition: enhancement tuned for human listeners can remove or distort cues a recogniser relies on, and so raise its word error rate. Live transcription hears exactly what you send, so check the recognition result on the test page before relying on Ours with transcription.
      • Ours has no automatic gain control, so your level may differ from Browser mode.

      How to test it

      1. Live in a meeting: join, then switch modes or toggle the switches while someone else listens. The lines under the switches show the active mode and what the browser reports for echoCancellation, noiseSuppression and autoGainControl. With Ours they also show the measured added latency and processing time. If a mode cannot start, the reason appears here and your previous mode stays on.
      2. Echo: use speakers, not headphones, while the other person talks. They listen for their own voice coming back; compare Off, Browser and Ours.
      3. Noise: make steady noise near your microphone (a fan, typing, music on another device) and ask the other person how each mode sounds.
      4. Two windows on one computer: open the invitation in a private window and join as a guest. Wear headphones or mute one window's microphone, or the two windows feed back into each other.
      5. Measured comparison: on the audio test page, record the on-screen script once per mode (stay silent for the 3-second countdown). Run the echo test with speakers at a fixed volume, and tick Play a noise clip to test noise. Run the recognition check to see the word error rate; it uploads your recordings to this server's recognizer, and only when you press it. The results table shows only measured values; repeat takes before drawing conclusions.
